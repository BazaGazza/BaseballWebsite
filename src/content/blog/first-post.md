A quick history about baseball, back in the 90s baseball was managed and played radically different. Scouts sported the wisdom and authority to find and recruit baseball prospects to join the big leagues. This had been the vetted process for years as scouts could evaluate the potential skill of an athlete and better yet directly observe all aspects of a player beyond their performance on the field. The core metrics evaluated were the batting average, runs batted in, and pitching wins. Big teams like the New York Yankees bolstered huge budgets exceeding 100 million dollars while other smaller teams had around less than half of that to cover all of their expenses. After a rookies big season they could come in and offer a ridiculous amount of money effectively swooping up all player talent with no limit and competing with them was an uphill battle. 

Introduce Billy Beane, a San Diego ex MLB player who had a knack for analyzing and assessing situations. After concluding his athletic career early, Billy became the general manager for the Oakland Athletics and began to build a winning team on a budget. Like mentioned before the team was never going to be able to afford top talent. He began to draft and consider on how to build the right team for the right price. The funny thing was that a lot of data was available just waiting idly for someone to come in and make sense of it especially with metrics other teams ignored. Introduce Paul DePodesta, Harvard economics graduate who joined Paul who joined Billy in their quest. Paul compiled and displayed sabermetrics were more reliable on predicting player performance such as the on-base percentage, slugging percentage, walk rate, and strikeout to walk ratio. Sabermetrics were originally coined by Bill James who also felt current metrics didn't capture the full story of a player. Better yet he displayed overlooked players that had been ignored for a variety of reasons allowing Oakland to secure them at a fraction of their expected price. It was essentially an untapped market that was up for grabs 

There was immediate backlash surrounding this new mentality shift as it challenged the established method of running a team. The universal sentiment was that this was a flawed approach and sooner or later the team would deflate. Billy and Paul drove the Moneyball approach throughout the seasons and it worked great as it drove the Oakland Athletics to their legendary 20 win streak in 2002 as the numbers they had evaluated when recruiting layers spoke for themselves. Unfortunately the win streak would be broken by the Minnesota Twins with pitcher Brad Radke performing an impressive six-hit game.

One thing they didn't loose on was on their balance sheet. During the 2002 season both the New York Yankees and Oakland Athletics tied wins with 103. However considering their payroll, the Oakland Athletics payed $388,390 dollars per win while the Yankees payed $1,222,608 dollars per win. Matching them up the ratio on price was 3.15 to 1. This meant that the Oakland Athletics payed a third dof the price for the same season performance thanks to the statistic backed moneyball approach. All MLB teams have since adopted statistical analysis in their management and for weighing in how they play the game. This pivotal era of baseball has been adapted into both a book and hit film starring Brad Pitt and Jonah Hill which we highly recommend for anyone that loves an underdog story. This project was inspired by the idea that baseball teams can use data to extract valuable key information. Teams can then use that information to make better decisions about attendance, pricing, and game-day strategy. By combining publicly available MLB data with analytics and predictive modeling, BAS aims to uncover patterns that help teams understand demand and improve the fan experience.
